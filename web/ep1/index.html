Rare Events · Part 1

The Mathematics of the Unlikely

Flip 1000 coins. What are the chances of 700 heads or more? The bell curve answers $8.5\times10^{-37}$; the truth is $8.8\times10^{-38}$. For 900 heads it is wrong by a factor $10^{20}$. This page lets you see why, with nothing more than coins and counting.

1One histogram, three lenses

Below is the exact probability of each possible number of heads out of $n$ coins (gold). Switch lenses to rescale the very same histogram:

  • Lens 1: the proportion of heads itself. As $n$ grows it piles up on ½: the law of large numbers.
  • Lens 2: zoom by $\sqrt n$ around ½. The shape freezes into the bell curve: the central limit theorem.
  • Lens 3: take $-\frac1n\ln P$. The shape freezes into a bowl $I(x)$, the rate function: $P(\text{proportion}\approx x)\approx e^{-nI(x)}$. That is large deviations.

In lens 3 the bell curve becomes the parabola $2(x-\tfrac12)^2$: it fits the bottom of the bowl and nothing else. The table compares, for the threshold you chose, the exact probability with the bell curve and with $e^{-nI(x)}$ (which gets the exponent right, up to a modest factor).

2Seeing the invisible: the rigged coin

Simulating fair coins never shows you a $10^{-37}$ event. The trick (importance sampling): flip a coin rigged to land heads with probability $q$, and give every run with $h$ heads and $t$ tails the weight $$w = \Big(\frac{0.5}{q}\Big)^{h}\Big(\frac{0.5}{1-q}\Big)^{t},$$ which undoes the cheat. The average of $w\times[\text{hit}]$ is an unbiased estimate of the fair-coin probability. The best $q$ is the one that makes the rare event typical: $q = k/n$.

3How does the unlikely happen? Conspiracy or culprit

Take 100 random amounts with average 1, and force their total to be $c$ times larger than usual. Ordinary amounts (exponential, like the cost of car accidents) share the extra burden: every amount grows a little, a conspiracy. Wild amounts (Pareto with exponent $\alpha$, like storm or earthquake damage) put it all on one culprit.

Ordinary amounts are drawn exactly given the total (they are then uniform on the simplex). Wild amounts are drawn by brute force: we generate batches of 100 until one has a total of at least $c\times100$.
